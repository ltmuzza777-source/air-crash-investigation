# Case 001: BlueWing Airlines Flight 96

## Status and scope

Status: Working story overview, subject to revision.

This is a fictional scenario, not a reconstruction of a real accident. No coding is included. The outline will be updated as evidence, performance, timing, and assessment are developed.

## Flight and setting

- Operator: BlueWing Airlines (fictional).
- Flight: 96.
- Route: Hobart, Tasmania, to Melbourne.
- Proposed aircraft: Airbus A320-232.
- Proposed year: 2012; exact date undecided.
- Intended configuration: Older PTU arrangement without the protective modification relevant to the chosen failure sequence; exact applicability and aircraft configuration require verification.
- Proposed landing runway: Hobart Runway 12.
- Proposed conditions: Daylight, dry runway, light headwind.
- Historical runway length: Approximately 2,251 metres before extension. Exact historical declared landing distance remains to be checked.

## Story summary

Shortly after takeoff, during landing-gear retraction, a flexible hydraulic hose associated with the right main landing-gear door ruptures. The leak depletes the green hydraulic system. Continued PTU operation contributes to yellow-system overheating. The crew isolates the affected yellow-system supply, leaving it unavailable during the immediate return.

Multiple warnings and high workload lead the crew to believe that a go-around would be unsafe. They make a rushed return to Hobart, continue a high and fast approach, and touch down late. Degraded stopping capability and insufficient remaining runway lead to an overrun.

Everyone survives. The aircraft sustains substantial damage.

## Intended event sequence

1. Flight 96 departs Hobart for Melbourne.
2. The initiating failure occurs after liftoff during gear retraction, rather than being fixed at V1.
3. A flexible green-system hose at the right main landing-gear door ruptures, causing a substantial external fluid leak.
4. Green hydraulic pressure is lost and gear retraction is disrupted.
5. PTU operation contributes to yellow-system overheating. Approximately two minutes is a provisional escalation interval, not a locked case timestamp.
6. Some relevant warnings appear after takeoff inhibition ends, increasing the crew's workload. Exact warning wording and order remain unverified.
7. The crew isolates the yellow-system supply in response to overheating. This is not necessarily a permanent yellow-system failure or a second fluid leak.
8. Both engines remain operating. The crew nevertheless believes that an immediate landing is necessary and a go-around would be unsafe.
9. The aircraft returns on a tight, rushed approach. Landing gear is down for touchdown; the exact extension sequence requires verification.
10. The approach remains high and fast. The aircraft floats and touches down well beyond the intended touchdown zone.
11. Reduced stopping capability combined with late touchdown results in a runway overrun.
12. The aircraft enters unpaved ground and sustains landing-gear and lower-fuselage damage. Everyone survives and no fire occurs.

## Initiating component failure

The hose rupture is the initiating failure. The PTU is not assumed to be broken: its operation is part of the escalation.

Proposed fictional examination finding: Localised deterioration at a repeatedly flexing section of hose.

The exact failure mechanism, hose identification, age, inspection history, and evidence distinguishing deterioration from impact damage remain provisional. No maintenance mistake is currently established as the initiating cause.

- Provisional touchdown point: Approximately 1,000 metres beyond the threshold.

The overrun must be supported by a consistent performance scenario. It must not be treated as an automatic consequence of hydraulic loss.

## Intended investigative questions

1. What component failed and why?
2. How did a green hydraulic leak lead to yellow-system overheating and temporary unavailability?
3. What stopping capability remained?
4. Why did the crew continue the unstable approach?
5. How did late touchdown and degraded stopping capability combine to produce the overrun?

The crew's belief that a go-around was unsafe is part of the fictional story, not proof that a go-around was technically impossible. Actual feasibility and relevant procedures must be checked before finalising that finding.
